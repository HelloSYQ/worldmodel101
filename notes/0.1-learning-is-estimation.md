# Lesson 0.1: Learning is estimation

> Phase 0 · Week 1 · Lab: [`labs/phase0/lab01_learning_is_estimation.py`](../labs/phase0/lab01_learning_is_estimation.py)

**Main idea.** Training a neural network, including every world model in this course, is maximum-likelihood estimation. The model is very flexible and has no closed-form solution, so gradient descent finds the parameters. The simplest case, a linear model with Gaussian noise, is ordinary least-squares linear regression, and it has a closed form we can check everything against.

---

## 1. MLE gives you the loss function

A model is a parameterized distribution $p_\theta(y\mid x)$. Training chooses $\theta$ to maximize the likelihood of the data:

```math
\theta^\star=\arg\max_\theta\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)
=\arg\min_\theta\underbrace{\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)\right)}_{\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;\text{"the loss"}}
```

Example: predict a house's price $y$ from a feature vector $\mathbf{x}$ (area, number of rooms, age). Assume the price is a linear function of the features plus Gaussian noise:

```math
y_i=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+\varepsilon_i,\quad \varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)
\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;
-\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\|^2+\text{const}
\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ML}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}
```

The rows of $\mathbf{X}$ are the houses. The last expression is the *normal equations*: maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise is least-squares linear regression.

## 2. Every loss is a noise assumption

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{Gaussian (fixed }\sigma\text{):}\quad & -\log p_\theta = \frac{\big(y-\mu_\theta(x)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\text{const} && \Rightarrow\ \text{MSE}\\[4pt]
\text{Laplace:}\quad & -\log p_\theta = \frac{\big|y-\mu_\theta(x)\big|}{b}+\text{const} && \Rightarrow\ \text{L1}\\[4pt]
\text{Bernoulli:}\quad & -\log p_\theta(y\mid x) = -\big[y\log s(z)+(1-y)\log\big(1-s(z)\big)\big],\;\; s(z)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}},\;\; z=f_\theta(x) && \Rightarrow\ \text{binary cross-entropy}\\[4pt]
\text{Categorical:}\quad & -\log p_\theta(y=k\mid x) = -\log\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},\;\; \mathbf{z}=f_\theta(x) && \Rightarrow\ \text{cross-entropy}\\[4pt]
\text{Gaussian (learned }\sigma\text{):}\quad & -\log p_\theta = \frac{\big(y-\mu_\theta(x)\big)^2}{2\sigma_\theta^2(x)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\log\sigma_\theta^2(x)+\text{const} && \Rightarrow\ \text{PETS-style loss (Phase 2)}
\end{aligned}
```

| Assumption | Loss | Optimal prediction | Typical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian, fixed $\sigma$ | MSE | the mean | house-price regression |
| Laplace | L1 | the median | regression with outliers |
| Bernoulli (sigmoid) | binary cross-entropy | the probability of class 1 | spam filter (logistic regression) |
| Categorical (softmax) | cross-entropy | a full distribution over classes | digit classification; next-token prediction in a language model |
| Gaussian, learned $\sigma_\theta(x)$ | Gaussian NLL | the mean plus its own uncertainty | a regressor that says how sure it is |

## 3. KL divergence, and why MLE minimizes it

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}\!\left[\log\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right]\;\ge\;0,
\qquad =0 \iff p=q,
\qquad \text{not symmetric}
```

```math
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\big[-\log p_\theta(y)\big]
=\underbrace{H(p_{\text{data}})}_{\text{independent of }\theta}
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_\theta\big)
```

So maximizing likelihood minimizes the **forward** KL; the left-hand side is the *cross-entropy*, hence the loss's name. The same quantities appear throughout information theory:

```math
H(p)=-\mathbb{E}_{p}[\log p(y)],
\qquad
I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)\,p(y)\big)
```

The two directions behave differently:

```math
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_\theta)}_{\text{forward: spreads to cover every mode (MLE)}}
\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi\,\|\,p)}_{\text{reverse: locks onto one mode (VAE, Dreamer)}}
```

If $p_\theta$ is a single Gaussian and the data has two modes, forward KL forces the Gaussian to stretch across both.

## 4. MAP estimation is regularization

```math
\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\min_\theta\left[-\sum_{i}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)-\log p(\theta)\right],
\qquad
p(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\tau^2\mathbf{I})\;\Rightarrow\;-\log p(\theta)=\frac{\|\theta\|^2}{2\tau^2}+\text{const}
```

The Gaussian prior adds an $\ell_2$ penalty: *ridge regression* in statistics, *weight decay* in deep learning. For linear regression:

```math
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}}
=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\|^2+\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\right]
=\left(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y},
\qquad \lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}
```

The regularization strength $\lambda$ is a ratio: how noisy the data is, relative to how large you believe the weights can be. A Laplace prior gives an $\ell_1$ penalty instead (the *lasso*), which pushes weights to exactly zero.

The classic demonstration is fitting a degree-9 polynomial to a few noisy samples of $\sin(2\pi x)$ (Bishop, §1.1). With 10 points, MLE passes through every one of them, with weights in the tens of millions, and swings wildly in between. The prior keeps the weights small and the curve smooth.

## 5. Gradient descent and SGD

When there is no closed form:

```math
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)
```

For linear regression:

```math
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2N}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\|^2,
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})
```

```math
\text{GD:}\quad \mathbf{w}_{k+1}=\mathbf{w}_k+\frac{\eta}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_k)
\qquad\qquad
\text{minibatch SGD:}\quad \mathbf{w}_{k+1}=\mathbf{w}_k+\frac{\eta}{|B_k|}\mathbf{X}_{B_k}^\top(\mathbf{y}_{B_k}-\mathbf{X}_{B_k}\mathbf{w}_k)
```

$B_k$ is a random subset of the training examples (a *minibatch*). Its gradient is a noisy but unbiased estimate of the full gradient: each step is much cheaper, but the iterate keeps jittering around the minimum unless $\eta$ decays. Deep networks are almost always trained this way.

Stability and speed: each eigen-direction of the error shrinks by $|1-\eta\lambda_i|$ per step, so

```math
\mathbf{R}=\nabla^2_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X},\qquad
0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{R})},\qquad
\eta^\star=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}},\qquad
\text{speed governed by } \kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}
```

**Feature scaling.** House area is in m² (40–250), the number of rooms is 1–8, the age is 0–80 years. On those raw scales $\mathbf{R}$ has eigenvalues of wildly different sizes, $\kappa$ is in the hundreds of thousands, and gradient descent crawls. Standardizing each feature (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) brings $\kappa$ down to about 20. It's the first step of almost every ML pipeline. Normalization layers and Adam treat the same problem inside deep networks.

A neural network replaces $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$ with a nonlinear $f_\theta(x)$: the loss becomes non-convex with no closed form, so we need gradient descent plus automatic differentiation (Lesson 0.2).

## 6. The blur problem

```math
\arg\min_{c}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-c)^2\big]=\mathbb{E}[Y],
\qquad
\arg\min_{c}\,\mathbb{E}\big[|Y-c|\big]=\operatorname{median}(Y)
```

```math
Y\in\{-1,+1\},\;\; P(Y=\pm1)=\tfrac12
\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0,\quad \text{yet } P(Y=0)=0
```

The MSE-optimal prediction is a value the world never produces. A model avoids this only by learning the whole distribution $p(Y)$ rather than a single point estimate.

## 7. Why this matters for world models

A world model is a conditional density, and training it is MLE on trajectories:

```math
p_\theta(o_{t+1}\mid o_{\le t},a_t),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{t}\log p_\theta(o_{t+1}\mid o_{\le t},a_t)
```

Almost every design choice in Phases 3–6 is a choice of
1. **which variable to model** (pixels, latents with reconstruction, latents without, task quantities only), and
2. **which distribution family to use**: a fixed-variance Gaussian gives MSE and blurry futures; stochastic latents (PlaNet, Dreamer), categorical tokens (IRIS, Genie) and diffusion (DIAMOND, GameNGen, Genie 3) all model $p(\text{future})$ instead of $\mathbb{E}[\text{future}]$.

## Lab 0.1 results

**A.1: three methods, one answer.** On a synthetic house-price dataset (500 training houses, noise standard deviation 25 k€), the closed form, gradient descent and SGD all reach a test RMSE of about 25 k€, the noise floor. Gradient descent matches the closed form to $10^{-11}$. SGD with a constant learning rate stays a little way off and keeps jittering:

| SGD, 10,000 steps | batch 1, constant $\eta$ | batch 32, constant $\eta$ | batch 1, decaying $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| relative distance to the closed form | $1\times10^{-2}$ | $6\times10^{-4}$ | $2\times10^{-3}$ |

The relative distance is $\|\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ML}}\|/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ML}}\|$. Bigger batches average the noise away; a decaying learning rate shrinks the jitter over time.

**A.2: step size and feature scaling** (standardized features).

| $\eta\,\lambda_{\max}$ | 0.5 | 1.0 | 1.9 | 2.1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| iterations to converge | 526 | 260 | 134 | diverges |

Here $\kappa\approx22$, so the best fixed step, $\eta^\star=2/(\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min})\approx1.91/\lambda_{\max}$, sits just below the stability limit. With raw features (m², rooms, years), $\kappa$ jumps from 22 to about 280,000 and gradient descent needs more than 200,000 iterations instead of 260.

**A.3: MLE vs MAP** (degree-9 polynomial, noisy $\sin(2\pi x)$, prior $w_j\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10^2)$; medians over 500 random datasets).

| Points $N$ | MLE RMSE | MAP RMSE | MLE largest $\lvert w_j\rvert$ | MAP largest $\lvert w_j\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 756 | 0.36 | $6\times10^{7}$ | 7 |
| 15 | 4.2 | 0.25 | $8\times10^{5}$ | 10 |
| 30 | 0.32 | 0.14 | $1\times10^{5}$ | 13 |
| 100 | 0.10 | 0.07 | $5\times10^{4}$ | 15 |

The prior helps a lot when data is scarce and matters less when there's plenty.

**B: the blur problem.** A car at an intersection turns left ($-1$) or right ($+1$), each with probability ½.

| Model | Prediction | Real futures within ±0.3 |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian NLL (MSE) | −0.001 | 0.0% |
| Laplace NLL (L1) | +0.664 | 17.6% |
| Categorical NLL (16 bins) | full distribution, both modes | samples: −0.9, +0.9, +1.1, … |
